Modernising ageing safety-critical electronics is often more cost-effective and environmentally sustainable than complete replacement, provided that functional equivalence with the original system can be demonstrated and the modernised system re-certified against contemporary standards. This case study, which continues prior work, addresses this challenge through the example of a legacy anti-skid test-diagnosis module used in European urban rail.

A legacy 8-bit controller board based on the Intel MCS-48 was retrofitted with a flash-based \texttt{Actel~A3P1000} \gls{fpga} emulating the original \gls{cpu} and \gls{io} logic, complemented by a galvanically isolated 32-bit Arm\textsuperscript{\textregistered}~\gls{mc}, an \texttt{STM32F401RET6}, for enhanced data logging. A central aim was a methodological testing framework suitable for broader application in safety-critical system retrofits. Its core is a two-layer verification strategy derived from the V-model:
\begin{enumerate*}[label=(\arabic*)]
	\item \textit{Component and Interface Tests:} Deterministic \gls{hdl} simulation, JTAG checks and hardware timing measurements examine logical correctness, basic hardware integrity and interface timing (e.g., watchdog, frequency sweep).
	\item \textit{System-Level Tests:} Scenario-based testing on a dedicated test fixture~\cite{dolata2023} with YAML-defined test cases examines integrated behaviour (fault memory, self-tests, diagnostics).
\end{enumerate*}
Simulation and integrated scenario testing indicate functional equivalence of the fault handling of the safety-critical core (fault detection, storage, display and read-out). The frequency sweep, however, deviates, and the deviation is open: in the only measurement against the original board, the emulation's sweep ends about 70~Hz lower (about 850~Hz against about 920~Hz), and by its design values it runs faster than the frequency change of about 180~ms that~\cite{dolata2023} gives for the original test card; settling the deviation needs a re-measurement of the original board or a check of its sweep circuit. Two hardware changes alter the module's behaviour by design: the STORE and RECALL inputs of the system connector no longer act on the fault memory, and an added brown-out detection can reset the emulated controller; their relevance in the vehicle is open. The evidence is not yet complete: the archived simulations and measurements date from 2023, before the rebuild; on the rebuilt unit, a follow-on team ran the fixture's test suite without an archived log, and several scenarios, among them the self-test, are not documented for that unit. Six integration defects documented during the rebuild are classified by how they were found; they did not come to light in a planned run of the two-layer method, and no single method found all of them. The layers were mapped to IEC~61508~Ed.~2, EN~50129:2018, EN~50716:2023 and IEEE~1012:2024 as whole standards, without an assessment of compliance; EN~50155:2021 remains future work. Although comprehensive \gls{hil} test rigs were beyond this project's scope, the approach borrows from \gls{hil} practice to facilitate future integration.

Refurbishing existing rolling-stock electronics can reduce costs and avoid the embodied emissions of manufacturing new components, and board-level retrofits share this rationale. A precedent is provided by an adaptive \gls{wsp} function that is also intended for existing fleets and has been certified against the current standard, EN~15595; validation of the methodology across further retrofit cases remains future work.
